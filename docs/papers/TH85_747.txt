ABSTRACT
We have constructed a model for the photodissociation region in Orion. This model matches the observed
emission in the fine-structure lines of O i 63 41m and 145 41m, C 1 609 /un, and C 11 158 41m and the low-lying
rotational lines of CO. It also explains the extended H2 1-0 S(l) emission, the carbon recombination lines, and
the C i 9850 Â line. The emission in the C 1 370 /tm, Si 11 35 /¿m, and Fe 11 26 /tm, 35 /¿m lines is predicted.
The model fit implies that a fairly uniform density (~105 cm-3) photodissociation region lies behind the Trapezium stars. The peak temperature in this region is about 500 K, and the intensity of the incident farultraviolet (FUV) field is approximately 105 times as intense as the ambient interstellar field. This model
shows that the observed high brightness temperature of the C 1 609 /mi line can be explained by emission
from the C+/C/CO transition region. This difference with previous chemical models is due to a higher gas
phase elemental abundance of carbon, to the charge exchange reactions of C+ with S and SiO, and to carbon
self-shielding.

I. INTRODUCTION
In a previous paper we have modeled the chemistry and
heating balance in molecular clouds illuminated by an intense
far-ultraviolet (FUV) (6 eV < hv < 13.6 eV) field (Tielens and
Hollenbach 1985, hereafter Paper I). The models assumed an
FUV-illuminated plane-parallel slab, so that the calculation
was strictly in one dimension, the depth measured in visual
extinction Av into the slab. Microturbulence with a characteristic Doppler width ôvd was assumed to dominate the pressure.
Constant hydrogen nucleus density n0 was assumed, so that
the pressure was approximately constant through the slab. The
numerical code solved for the equilibrium gas temperature T
and the chemistry as a function of Av. The dominant heating
mechanism was found to be the photoelectric ejection of energetic electrons from grains, which transforms up to 0.04 of the
incident FUV flux into gas heating. This “ heating efficiency ”
depends on the positive charge of the grains, which in turn
depends on a unitless parameter y cc GqT0 5^1, with ne the
electron number density and G0 the FUV flux measured in
units of the “Habing field,” which is taken to be 1.6 x 10-3
ergs cm-2 s_ 1 (Habing 1968). The emergent spectrum from the
slab was calculated using an escape probability formalism for
the line radiation. The predicted spectra included the finestructure lines of O i, C i, C 11, Fe 11, and Si 11; the low-lying
rotational transitions of CO, and an estimate of the FUVpumped H2 1-0 2 41m vibrational transitions. The spectra
depended mainly on the model free parameters n0 and G0.
Other model parameters which generally can be derived from
other observations or theoretical considerations include the
gaseous elemental abundances, c)i;d, the ratio of FUV opacity
to optical opacity of the grains, the ratio of Av to column
density of hydrogen nuclei, and the emergent far-infrared continuum flux from dust, FIR, which can be calculated from the
dust temperature T0 and the 100 41m dust optical depth t100 Mm.
The results of the models show that the FUV creates a hot
(100-1000 K) layer (Av < 3) of atomic gas (H, O, C+). This gas
is heated by photoelectrons from dust grains and cooled by the

O i 63 41m and C 11 158 /im fine-structure lines. Around Av »
3-4 atomic hydrogen and ionized carbon are transformed into
H2 and C or CO, respectively. This warm (50-100 K) gas is
also heated by photoelectrons from dust grains but is cooled by
molecular rotation lines from CO. Beyond Av> 4 carbon is
mainly in the form of CO. The gas is now heated by the infrared continuum from warm (~75 K) dust grains. Ground state
atomic oxygen is radiatively excited by 63 /tm continuum
photons, followed by collisional de-excitation by H2 molecules
which heats the gas. The gas is also heated by collisions with
warm dust grains. Cooling still occurs through the CO rotational lines. Deep in the cloud (Av >10) atomic oxygen is converted into 02.
These models are relevant not only to the gas which
separates H 11 regions from their parental molecular cloud but
also to neutral shells around planetary nebulae, bright-rimmed
clouds, reflection nebulae, and regions surrounding protostars.
In this paper we will apply our model to the well-studied Orion
photodissociation region. Applications to the other regions are
deferred to future publications.
This paper is organized as follows. Section II summarizes the
observations of Orion. In § III we analyze the fine-structure
observations. The emphasis is on a simple semianalytic model
which highlights the essential physics. In this way values for
physical parameters of this region such as density, temperature,
electron density, and intensity of the incident FUV field are
derived. The observations are also compared with our detailed
numerical model calculations, and a best fit model is constructed. Section IV summarizes the main results.

The parameters of the standard model in Paper I have been
chosen to mimic the conditions expected for the photodissociation region directly behind 91 Ori C (Table 2). The calculated emergent line intensities for the standard model are
compared with the observations in Table 1. Also shown in this
table are the results of a possible best fit model. This model has

similar to that in the standard model (cf. Fig. 9 of Paper I). The
column density of most species are, however, larger by a factor
of 2 because of the increased penetration depth of the FUV.

will allow us to calculate the global physical parameters of the
region (e.g., T, n0, and G0) from the observations.

larger than the lower limit to the density estimated from the
extent of the 10 and 20 /un emission associated with the ionization bar (Becklin et al 1976). With our estimated density the
physical extent of the atomic region emitting the 10 and 20 /un

streaming away of material from the ionization front.

formation of the infrared cluster in the BN/KL region has

further modified at the front of the molecular cloud by the

b) The Cu 15& fim Line
The observed intensity of the C n 158 /un line is relatively
easy to analyze. Because the density in the photodissociation
region is much larger than the critical density, the level populations are given by the Boltzmann distribution function.
Because the temperature in the C+ zone is much larger than
the excitation energy (92 K), the level populations are only
weakly dependent on T. Thus, for an optically thin line, the
C ii 158 /¿m intensity is mainly dependent on the total C +
column density. For an optically thick line the C n 158 jm\
intensity is given by the Planck function at the kinetic temperature (cf. eq. [1]). In Paper I a simple analytical model was
used to derive the intensity of the C n 158 jum line as a function of the total C+ column density and the temperature (see
Fig. 5 in Paper I). For our best estimate of turbulent Doppler
width SvD = 2.7 km s-1 in the photodissociation region, the
observed C n 158 jam intensity implies a temperature of at
least ~250 K, in good agreement with values estimated from
the O i 63 /¿m line. The observations also imply a total C +
column density of about 5 x 1018 cm-2.
The column density of C + is mainly determined by the penetration of FUV photons into the slab. Typically, the C +
region extends to a grain optical depth at À ~ 1000 Â of

density can be raised by two more probable causes. First, the
dust in molecular clouds may not have “ normal ” properties.

increasing the extent of the C+ region. In order to explain the
intensities observed in Orion, the slab has to be tilted by about
60°.
These simple analytical considerations agree well with our
extensive numerical calculations. The C n 158 fim intensity in
the standard model falls short of the observations by about a
factor of 2 (Table 2). The C II 158 jj,m line intensity in the
possible best fit model, which has a lower dust-to-gas ratio
than the standard model, is in much better agreement with the
observations. It should be emphasized, however, that an
equally good fit to the observations can be obtained by tilting
the slab or by choosing a normal gas-to-dust ratio combined
with a gray FUV dust opacity (t/Av = 1). At present we cannot
rule out any of these possibilities, and it is possible that all are
operating. We do note, however, that the observed variations
in the C ii 158 /xm line intensities in Orion are probably due to
variations in geometry rather than to dust properties.

The column density of neutral carbon in the photodissociation region model is about 10 times larger than previous calculations indicated (Glassgold and Langer 1975;
Langer 1976, 1977; de Jong, Dalgarno, and Boland 1980). As

The low column densities of atomic carbon predicted by
previous gas-phase chemistry models failed to provide the
observed C i 609 /mi intensities. The failure of these chemical
equilibrium models has led to several speculations in the literature on the origin of the high atomic carbon column densities
inferred from observation.
It has been suggested that the high atomic carbon column
densities are caused by the lack of chemical equilibrium in
molecular clouds. The chemical equilibrium time scale inside a
dense cloud, where the chemistry is dominated by cosmic-ray
ionization, is about 106 yr. Starting with an atomic cloud (i.e.,
all carbon in C+), large abundances of C i are present for
t< 106 yr in time-dependent models (Iglesias 1977; Gerola
and Glassgold 1978; Graedel, Langer, and Frerking 1982;
Tarafdar et al 1985). However, this young implied age for
molecular clouds is in conflict with other observations which
suggest ages of 107-108 yr (cf. Bash and Peters 1976; Bash,
Green, and Peters 1977). In particular, the presence of several
subassociations suggest a lifetime of the Orion molecular cloud
larger than 107 yr (Elmegreen and Lada 1977).
The nonequilibrium of the molecular cloud might be caused
by turbulence. Boland and de Jong (1982) suggest that churning of atomic material from the surface to the center of the
cloud leads to large amounts of C i, because of the photodissociation of CO at the surface and the long time scale for
re-formation of CO in the center. However, it should be noted
that an FUV radiation field capable of photodissociating CO is
also capable of photoionizing C to C + . Furthermore, the
chemical equilibrium time scale in the presence of an FUV field
is much shorter than the 106 yr quoted above for the interiors
of dense clouds (a fact noted by Boland and de Jong 1982). As
estimated in Paper I, the chemical equilibrium time scale in
photodissociation regions is only 104 yr, much shorter than the
turbulent circulation time scale. Consequently, photodissociation regions are expected to be in chemical equi-

librium, and the carbon brought to the interior by turbulence
should be mainly in the form of CO. Indeed, detailed model
calculations of Boland and de Jong (1984) show atomic carbon
in the photodissociation region of a dark molecular cloud, but
the center is entirely CO.
Our photodissociation model for the C i 609 /un emission
places the C i in chemical equilibrium at the cloud surface
(Av < 10). The emission is barely optically thin (t ~ 1) and
originates in a warm (50 K) molecular gas. Our models predict
about 10 times greater atomic carbon column densities than
previous theoretical models and match the observationally
inferred (optically thin) lower limits on C column densities. In
the remainder of this section we argue for these lower limits on
the observed C i column densities rather than the higher values
favored by Phillips and Huggins (1981). They propose that
C i 609 fim is very optically thick, based on a curve-of-growth
analysis.
The emission from the C i 609 /¿m line is very extended
( ~ 25'), and its brightness temperature is about equal to that of
the 12CO J = 1-0 line in the outer regions which lie > 10' from
01 Ori C. In the central region the brightness temperature of
the C i 609 /¿m line is only about one-third that of the 12CO
J = 1-0 line. In our model this behavior of the C i 609 /¿m line
is caused by an increase in optical depth with distance from
Q1 Ori C. In the central region the C i 609 /im line has an
optical depth < 1 in our model; in the outer regions this line is
optically thick. This difference in optical depth is caused not by
an increase in the C i column density but rather by a decreased
turbulent Doppler width. The C i 609 /mi line width decreases
from about 5 km s_ 1 in the central region to about 2 km s-1
25' away (T. G. Phillips 1984, private communication). Therefore, in the outer regions where they are both optically thick,
the 12CO J = 1-0 and C i 609 /¿m lines measure essentially the
kinetic gas temperature and are insensitive to n0 and G0.
Phillips and Huggins (1981) have suggested that the
C i 609 jim line is very optically thick even in the center of the
OMC-1 region. This conclusion was reached on the basis of a
comparison of its line width to that of the 12CO lines and its
isotopes. We feel that such a curve-of-growth analysis of this
complex region may be misleading. The presence of temperature, density, and turbulent or systematic velocity gradients can lead to erroneous conclusions based on line-width
comparisons. For example, the C i 609 /on and 13CO J = 2-1
lines are both optically thick in the central region (tc ! ~ 25),
according to the analysis of Phillips and Huggins (1981), yet
their brightness temperatures are much less than the kinetic
gas temperature derived from the J = 1-0 12CO line (t ~ 80).
In our opinion, this difficulty stems from the presence of two
regions superposed on the sky: (1) the warm OMC-1 core
around BN/KL, including the warm photodissociation region
behind the Trapezium, and (2) the cold, lower density molecular gas behind BN/KL (see Fig. 1). These regions have quite
different densities, temperatures, column densities, and intrinsic line widths. The H2CO and CO observations, for example,
show complex velocity structure (Bastien et al 1981; Schloerb
and Loren 1982). The H2CO line width (FWHM) is only
1-2 km s-1, which may reflect quiescent conditions much
deeper into the Orion molecular cloud, far beyond the photodissociation region. Therefore, we believe that the colder
quiescent region has an intrinsic line width (FWHM) of about
1-2 km s~\ while the region around and in front of BN/KL
has a line width (FWHM) of about 5 km s -1 (or ôvd = 2.7 km
s_1), as inferred from the carbon recombination lines. The CO

IV. SUMMARY
We have applied our theoretical model, which calculates the
energy balance and chemical equilibrium of dense molecular
clouds illuminated by high FUV fields, to the photodissociation region in Orion. Our model can explain the
observed intensities in the O i 63 pm and 145 pm,
C ii 158 pm, C i 609 pm, and the low-lying rotational transitions of CO. It also explains the extended vibrationally
excited H2 emission, the carbon radio recombination lines, and
the far-red C i 9850 Â emission.
We have illustrated how the O i 63 pm, C n 158 pm, and IR
continuum maps can be used, without the detailed thermochemical modeling described in Paper I, to probe the physical
state of the photodissociation region. The ratio of the sum of

A detailed thermochemical one-dimensional model fit to the
observed emission from the photodissociation region associated with the Trapezium in Orion is obtained with a gas
density of n0 = 105 cm-3 and an incident FUV flux 105 times
the average interstellar value. The FUV flux implies that
61 Ori C. lies about 0.2 pc from the molecular cloud. The gas
density in the photodissociation region is relatively constant to
a distance <3' from 61 Ori C. The dense (~ 106 cm-3) molecular ridge at BN/KL must lie buried deeper (Av > 10) in the
cloud. The match to the extended H2 1-0 S(l) emission implies
that H2 formation occurs efficiently on grains with temperatures Tgr ~ 75 K.
The large extent of the O i 63 /mi emission is a natural consequence of the photoelectric heating mechanism. When the
FUV flux drops, the positive grain charge decreases, resulting

in a larger heating efficiency per FUV photon. As a consequence, the intensity of the O i 63 /im line, the dominant
cooling line of the gas, does not drop as fast as the far-infrared
continuum with distance from the exciting star.
Most of the C i 609 ¿mi emission and the 12CO 1-0 emission
observed toward OMC-1 originates in the photodissociation
region. The 12CO 1-0 transition becomes optically thick at
Av ~ 4, and the peak brightness temperature measures the gas
temperature at this depth. The C i 609 /im emission is marginally optically thin, so that its peak brightness temperature
( ~ 20 K) lies below the gas temperature. We argue that it is not
necessary to invoke nonequilibrium chemistry, and therefore
extremely young ages <106 years, in order to explain C i
abundances observed toward molecular clouds. To explain the
CO millimeter emission, we propose a two-component model
with a warm foreground turbulent region (which includes the
photodissociation region) and a cold background quiescent
region. Some of the CO lines (e.g., 12CO 1-0 and 2-1 and 13CO
2-1) come from the turbulent region; others (e.g., 13CO 1-0
and 12C180 and 2-1) come from the cold molecular cloud.
